# 🌍 Distribution of Water

Water is one of Earth's most important natural resources.

About **71% of the Earth's surface** is covered with water. However, only **3%** of this water is **fresh water** and potentially usable by humans.

Out of this **3%**:

- About **2%** is locked in glaciers and ice caps.
- Only about **1%** is available in rivers, lakes, streams, swamps, and other freshwater sources that humans can access more easily.

> [!NOTE]
> Although Earth appears to have plenty of water, only a **very small proportion is readily available for human use.**

---

## Global Distribution of Fresh Water

There is enough water on Earth for every human being, either directly or indirectly.

The main problem is that **fresh water is not distributed evenly** around the world.

- Around **half of the world's freshwater** is found in only **six countries**.
- Much of this water still requires **purification** before it is safe for drinking.
- In many developing countries, people—especially women—may have to walk several kilometres each day to collect water for their families.

This unequal distribution creates major social and economic challenges.

---

# 💧 The Water Cycle

The **water cycle** is the continuous movement of water between the atmosphere, land, and oceans.

Water changes both **its state** (solid, liquid, gas) and **its location** as it moves through the cycle.

For example, water stored in a glacier can melt, flow into rivers, evaporate, and eventually return as rainfall.

---

## Stages of the Water Cycle

1. Water evaporates from rivers, lakes, and oceans due to the Sun's heat.
2. Water vapour rises into the atmosphere.
3. The vapour cools and undergoes **condensation**, forming clouds.
4. **Precipitation** occurs as rain, snow, or hail.
5. Water returns to the Earth's surface.

Once precipitation reaches land, it can follow several different pathways:

- Flow directly into rivers and eventually the sea.
- Be absorbed by plants and later returned to the atmosphere through **transpiration**.
- Flow across the surface as **surface run-off**.
- Be intercepted by plant leaves (**interception**) before later evaporating or undergoing transpiration.
- Enter the ground through **infiltration**.

---

## Movement of Water Underground

After **infiltration**, water may continue moving underground.

There are two main pathways:

| Process | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Throughflow** | Water moves horizontally through the soil. |
| **Groundwater flow** | Water moves through deeper layers of porous rock after deep infiltration. |

> [!TIP]
> Remember:
>
> - **Infiltration** = Water enters the soil.
> - **Throughflow** = Water moves through the soil.
> - **Groundwater flow** = Water moves through rocks underground.

---

## Quick Revision

- **71%** of Earth is covered by water.
- Only **3%** is fresh water.
- About **2%** is locked in glaciers.
- Only **1%** is readily available for human use.
- The water cycle continuously moves water between the atmosphere, land, and oceans.
- Key processes include **evaporation, condensation, precipitation, infiltration, throughflow, groundwater flow, transpiration, interception, and surface run-off**.

---

### Exam Tip

> [!TIP]
> Cambridge frequently asks students to **label or explain the water cycle**. Learn the sequence of the processes and be able to distinguish between **infiltration**, **throughflow**, and **groundwater flow**.

---

### Common Exam Mistakes

> [!WARNING]
> - Saying that **3%** of Earth's water is easily available—most of it is actually locked in glaciers.
> - Confusing **evaporation** with **transpiration**.
> - Mixing up **infiltration** (water entering the soil) with **groundwater flow** (water moving through rocks underground).

# 🚰 Daily Uses of Water

Water is essential for human survival and economic development.

Humans use water for **three main purposes**:

1. Domestic use
2. Industrial use
3. Agricultural use

---

## 1. Domestic Use

Domestic water is used in homes for everyday activities such as:

- Drinking
- Cooking
- Washing
- Bathing
- Cleaning
- Sanitation

Safe, clean water is essential to prevent the spread of disease.

> [!NOTE]
> Economically developed countries (EDCs) generally use **much more domestic water per person** than less economically developed countries (LEDCs).

---

## 2. Industrial Use

Many industries require large quantities of water during manufacturing.

Common industrial uses include:

- Cooling machinery
- Washing equipment
- Producing steam
- Chemical processing
- Manufacturing products

Industries that are especially dependent on water include:

- Pharmaceutical industries
- Textile industries
- Beverage industries
- Energy production

---

## 3. Agricultural Use

Agriculture is the **largest consumer of freshwater**, accounting for approximately **70% of global freshwater use**.

Water is used for:

- Irrigating crops
- Providing drinking water for livestock
- Cleaning equipment and farm buildings

As the world's population continues to grow, agricultural demand for water is also increasing.

---

# 🌊 Sources of Fresh Water

Fresh water is obtained from both **surface water** and **groundwater** sources.

Where freshwater is scarce, **desalinated seawater** may also be used.

---

## 1. Rivers and Reservoirs

Rivers are one of the most important sources of freshwater.

They can be used to:

- Supply drinking water.
- Irrigate farmland.
- Generate hydroelectric power.
- Store water behind dams in **reservoirs**.

River water is usually **treated** before being supplied for human consumption.

> [!TIP]
> A **service (elevated) reservoir** stores treated drinking water before it is distributed to homes and businesses. Water towers are common examples.

---

## 2. Groundwater

Some rainwater infiltrates into the ground and collects within **porous rocks**, forming underground water stores known as **aquifers**.

Groundwater is extracted using:

- Wells
- Electric pumps
- Buckets

### Artesian Wells

An **artesian well** taps into an aquifer where water is naturally under pressure.

Because of this pressure, water may rise to the surface without the need for pumping.

---

## 3. Seawater

In regions where freshwater is limited, seawater can be converted into drinking water through **desalination**.

Desalination removes dissolved salts, making water **potable** (safe to drink).

---

## Methods of Desalination

### 1. Distillation

Distillation involves:

1. Heating seawater until it evaporates.
2. Collecting the water vapour.
3. Cooling the vapour so it condenses into fresh water.

**Advantages**

- Produces very pure water.

**Disadvantages**

- Expensive.
- Requires large amounts of energy.
- Produces large quantities of concentrated salt water (**brine**).

---

### 2. Reverse Osmosis

Reverse osmosis forces seawater through very fine membranes under **high pressure**.

The membranes allow water molecules to pass through while blocking dissolved salts.

**Advantages**

- More energy efficient.
- Less expensive than distillation.
- Produces less brine.
- More widely used today.

**Disadvantages**

- Requires specialised membranes.
- Membranes require regular maintenance and replacement.

---

## Comparison of Desalination Methods

| Distillation | Reverse Osmosis |
| :--- | :--- |
| Water is boiled and condensed. | Water is forced through fine membranes. |
| High energy requirement. | Lower energy requirement. |
| More expensive. | Less expensive. |
| Produces more brine. | Produces less brine. |
| Less efficient. | More efficient. |

---

## Quick Revision

- Water is used for **domestic, industrial, and agricultural purposes**.
- Agriculture accounts for **around 70%** of freshwater use.
- Freshwater comes from **rivers, reservoirs, groundwater, and aquifers**.
- Seawater can be made potable through **desalination**.
- **Reverse osmosis** is generally more efficient than **distillation**.

---

### Exam Tip

> [!TIP]
> Cambridge often asks students to **compare distillation and reverse osmosis**. Learn at least **three clear differences** between the two methods.

---

### Common Exam Mistakes

> [!WARNING]
> - Confusing **aquifers** with **artesian wells**. An aquifer stores groundwater, while an artesian well is a way of accessing pressurised groundwater.
> - Saying desalination creates freshwater naturally—it is an **artificial treatment process**.
> - Forgetting that **agriculture** uses the largest share of global freshwater.
---

# 🌍 Availability of Safe Drinking Water

Although Earth has enough water to meet human needs, **safe drinking water is not equally available** around the world.

A country's **water wealth** is often determined by the amount of rainfall it receives.

Examples include:

- **Water-wealthy countries:** Russia and Brazil
- **Water-poor countries:** United Arab Emirates (UAE) and Kuwait

However, receiving plenty of rainfall does **not** guarantee that everyone has access to safe drinking water.

Many countries still struggle because of limited infrastructure, poor management, or economic constraints.

---

## Types of Water Scarcity

There are **two main types of water scarcity**.

### 1. Physical Water Scarcity

Physical water scarcity occurs when there is **not enough naturally available freshwater**.

Common causes include:

- Low rainfall
- High evaporation rates
- Desert climates
- Climate change

**Examples:** UAE, Kuwait

---

### 2. Economic Water Scarcity

Economic water scarcity occurs when a country **has water available but lacks the money or infrastructure** to:

- Extract it.
- Purify it.
- Store it.
- Transport it to people.

Many developing countries experience this form of water scarcity despite receiving adequate rainfall.

---

## Comparison of Water Scarcity

| Physical Water Scarcity | Economic Water Scarcity |
| :--- | :--- |
| Caused by lack of freshwater resources. | Caused by lack of money, technology, or infrastructure. |
| Usually occurs in dry climates. | Can occur even where water is abundant. |
| Related to environmental conditions. | Related to economic development and management. |

---

## Unequal Access to Water

Water shortages do not only occur **between countries**.

Access to clean water can also vary:

- Between different regions of the same country.
- Between urban and rural areas.
- Between developed and underdeveloped communities.

These differences may lead to:

- Competition for water resources.
- Political disagreements.
- Social conflicts.

> [!NOTE]
> In Pakistan, disagreements over water allocation between provinces—particularly **Punjab and Sindh**—highlight how unequal water distribution can become a significant national issue.

---

# 🏞️ Multipurpose Dam Projects

A **multipurpose dam** is a dam designed to provide **several benefits** rather than serving only one purpose.

Although dam projects require large investments and careful planning, they play a vital role in water management.

---

## Advantages of Multipurpose Dams

Multipurpose dams provide many benefits, including:

-  Hydroelectric power (HEP) generation.
-  Flood control.
-  Irrigation for agriculture.
-  Drinking water supply.
-  Habitats for fish and other aquatic organisms.
-  Employment opportunities.
-  Tourism and recreation.

---

## Disadvantages of Multipurpose Dams

Despite their benefits, dams also have several environmental and social impacts.

These include:

- Displacement of local communities.
- Temporary flooding when spillways are opened.
- Disruption of fish migration and breeding.
- Reduced water flow downstream.
- Trapping of sediment and silt.
- Gradual weakening of dam structures due to silt accumulation.
- Increased risk of seismic activity in some regions.

---

## Choosing a Suitable Dam Site

Engineers carefully evaluate several factors before constructing a dam.

An ideal location generally has:

- A narrow valley.
- High elevation.
- Stable rock foundations.
- An isolated area with fewer people to relocate.

Careful planning helps maximise the benefits while reducing environmental impacts.

---

## Are Dams Sustainable?

Multipurpose dams have both sustainable and unsustainable characteristics.

### Sustainable Benefits

- Produce renewable hydroelectric power.
- Reduce dependence on fossil fuels.
- Store water for future use.
- Support irrigation and domestic water supply.
- Can provide habitats for aquatic organisms.
- Offer long-term economic benefits.

### Environmental Concerns

- Siltation reduces reservoir capacity over time.
- Waterlogging may occur upstream.
- Fish migration routes are disrupted.
- Temporary flooding affects nearby communities.
- Increased seismic activity has been reported around some reservoirs.

---

## Quick Revision

- Water availability differs greatly around the world.
- **Physical water scarcity** is caused by limited natural water resources.
- **Economic water scarcity** is caused by insufficient infrastructure or funding.
- Multipurpose dams provide electricity, irrigation, flood control, and water storage.
- Dams also have environmental and social disadvantages, including displacement, habitat disruption, and silt accumulation.

---

### Exam Tip

> [!TIP]
> Cambridge frequently asks students to **evaluate multipurpose dam projects**. Always include **both advantages and disadvantages** to earn full marks.

---

### Common Exam Mistakes

> [!WARNING]
> - Confusing **physical** and **economic** water scarcity.
> - Writing only the benefits of dams while ignoring their environmental impacts.
> - Assuming countries with high rainfall automatically have universal access to safe drinking water.

# 🦠 Water-related Diseases

Unsafe or contaminated water is responsible for the spread of many diseases around the world.

These diseases can be grouped into **two main categories**:

1. **Water-borne diseases** – spread through contaminated water.
2. **Water-related diseases** – spread by organisms that depend on water for part of their life cycle.

---

# 1. Water-borne Diseases

Water-borne diseases are caused by **pathogenic microorganisms** that are present in contaminated water.

Poor sanitation can allow **sewage** to enter rivers, lakes, and other water sources, leading to the spread of disease.

The two major water-borne diseases in the Cambridge syllabus are:

- **Cholera**
- **Typhoid**

Both are caused by **bacteria** and spread when people drink contaminated water.

---

## Causes

Water-borne diseases commonly result from:

- Poor sanitation.
- Untreated sewage entering water bodies.
- Drinking untreated or contaminated water.
- Poor personal hygiene.

---

## Prevention

These diseases can be prevented by:

- Providing safe drinking water.
- Proper sewage treatment.
- Good sanitation systems.
- Maintaining personal hygiene.
- Boiling contaminated water before drinking.

> [!TIP]
> Simply **boiling water** is one of the easiest and most effective methods of killing disease-causing bacteria.

---

# 2. Water-related Diseases

Unlike water-borne diseases, water-related diseases are **not spread directly through drinking water**.

Instead, they are spread by **vectors** that depend on water to complete part of their life cycle.

The main example is **malaria**.

---

## Malaria

Malaria is caused by a microscopic parasite called **Plasmodium**.

The parasite lives inside **female Anopheles mosquitoes**, which breed in **stagnant water**.

When an infected mosquito bites a person, it transfers the parasite into the bloodstream.

The mosquito acts as a **vector**.

> **Vector:** An organism that carries and transmits a disease-causing organism from one host to another.

---

## Symptoms of Malaria

Common symptoms include:

- Fever
- Chills
- Flu-like illness
- Sweating
- Weakness

Without treatment, malaria can become life-threatening.

---

## Prevention by Individuals

People can reduce the risk of malaria by:

- Avoiding outdoor activities at night.
- Wearing long-sleeved clothing.
- Using mosquito repellents.
- Sleeping under insecticide-treated mosquito nets.
- Spraying insecticides inside homes.

---

## Government Control Measures

Governments can help control malaria by:

- Spraying insecticides in buildings.
- Draining stagnant water and wetlands where mosquitoes breed.
- Introducing fish that feed on mosquito larvae into ponds and reservoirs.

---

## Water-borne vs Water-related Diseases

| Water-borne Diseases | Water-related Diseases |
| :--- | :--- |
| Spread by contaminated water. | Spread by vectors associated with water. |
| Caused by drinking polluted water. | Caused by bites from infected organisms. |
| Examples: Cholera, Typhoid | Example: Malaria |
| Usually bacterial diseases. | Caused by the *Plasmodium* parasite carried by mosquitoes. |

---

## Quick Revision

- **Cholera** and **Typhoid** are **water-borne diseases** caused by contaminated water.
- **Malaria** is a **water-related disease** spread by mosquitoes.
- Mosquitoes act as **vectors**.
- Good sanitation, safe drinking water, and proper sewage treatment prevent water-borne diseases.
- Mosquito control helps reduce malaria transmission.

---

### Exam Tip

> [!TIP]
> Cambridge often asks students to **compare water-borne and water-related diseases**. Remember that **cholera and typhoid are spread through contaminated water**, whereas **malaria is transmitted by a mosquito vector**.

---

### Common Exam Mistakes

> [!WARNING]
> - Saying malaria is spread by drinking dirty water—it is **spread by mosquito bites**.
> - Forgetting that mosquitoes are **vectors**, not the actual cause of malaria.
> - Mixing up **cholera** and **typhoid** with malaria in comparison questions.
---

# 🏭 Sources, Impacts and Management of Water Pollution

Water pollution occurs when harmful substances enter rivers, lakes, oceans, or groundwater, reducing water quality and harming living organisms.

The main sources of water pollution are:

1. Domestic waste
2. Industrial processes
3. Agriculture

---

# 1. Domestic Waste (Sewage)

**Sewage** is wastewater produced by households, businesses, and communities.

It contains:

- Human waste
- Food waste
- Organic matter
- Soap and detergents
- Harmful microorganisms

Sewage is transported through underground pipes called **sewers** to a **sewage treatment plant**, where it is treated before being released into the environment.

---

# 🧪 Sewage Treatment

The main purpose of sewage treatment is to:

- Remove harmful microorganisms.
- Reduce pollution.
- Lower the **Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD)** of wastewater before it enters rivers or lakes.

> [!NOTE]
> **Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD)** is the amount of oxygen that microorganisms need to decompose organic matter in water.
>
> A **high BOD** means microorganisms consume large amounts of oxygen, leaving less oxygen available for fish and other aquatic organisms.

---

## Stages of Sewage Treatment

### Step 1 — Collection

Wastewater from homes and industries is transported through sewers to a sewage treatment plant.

---

### Step 2 — Screening

Large objects such as:

- Plastic
- Wood
- Cloth
- Rubbish

are removed using screens.

---

### Step 3 — Primary Sedimentation

The wastewater enters a settling tank.

Heavy solid organic matter settles to the bottom, forming **sludge**.

The sludge is removed and sent to a **sludge digester**.

---

### Step 4 — Aeration

Air (oxygen) is bubbled through the wastewater.

This encourages the growth of aerobic bacteria, which break down dissolved organic matter.

As a result:

- Pollution decreases.
- Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD) is reduced.

---

### Step 5 — Secondary Sedimentation

The bacteria and remaining sludge settle to the bottom.

Part of the **activated sludge** is transferred to the sludge digester.

The cleaner water continues to the next stage.

---

### Step 6 — Effluent Discharge

The treated water, known as **effluent**, is released into rivers or other water bodies.

It is much cleaner and less harmful than untreated sewage.

---

### Step 7 — Sludge Digestion

The collected sludge is placed in a digester under **oxygen-free (anaerobic) conditions**.

Anaerobic bacteria break down the sludge and produce **methane gas**.

Methane can be collected and used as a renewable energy source.

---

### Step 8 — Reuse of Sludge

The treated sludge is rich in nutrients and organic matter.

It can be safely used as an **organic fertilizer**.

---

## Tertiary Treatment

Some sewage treatment plants carry out **tertiary treatment**.

This involves:

- Additional filtration.
- Chlorination (disinfection).

This produces cleaner water and further reduces harmful microorganisms.

---

# 🚰 Producing Potable Water

**Potable water** is water that is safe for human consumption.

To make untreated water potable, it undergoes several treatment stages:

1. **Coagulation** – chemicals cause tiny particles to clump together.
2. **Filtration** – removes suspended particles.
3. **Disinfection (Chlorination)** – kills harmful bacteria and pathogens.

---

## Sewage Treatment Flow Summary

| Stage | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- |
| Screening | Removes large solid waste. |
| Primary sedimentation | Removes sludge. |
| Aeration | Bacteria decompose organic matter. |
| Secondary sedimentation | Removes bacteria and remaining sludge. |
| Effluent discharge | Releases treated water into the environment. |
| Sludge digestion | Produces methane and nutrient-rich sludge. |
| Tertiary treatment (optional) | Further filtration and chlorination. |

---

## Quick Revision

- Sewage contains organic waste and microorganisms.
- Sewage treatment reduces **Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD)**.
- Aerobic bacteria break down organic matter during aeration.
- Sludge is treated anaerobically to produce methane.
- Treated sludge can be used as fertilizer.
- Potable water is produced through **coagulation, filtration, and chlorination**.

---

### Exam Tip

> [!TIP]
> Cambridge frequently asks students to **describe the stages of sewage treatment** or explain **why BOD decreases after treatment**. Learn the treatment sequence in order and remember the purpose of each stage.

---

### Common Exam Mistakes

> [!WARNING]
> - Confusing **sewage treatment** with **potable water treatment**. They are different processes.
> - Forgetting that **aerobic bacteria require oxygen** during aeration.
> - Mixing up **sludge** (solid waste) with **effluent** (treated liquid released into the environment).
> - Thinking methane is a waste product—it can be collected and used as a renewable energy source.
---

# 🏭 Industrial Water Pollution

Industries use and produce many chemicals that can pollute the environment if they are not properly treated before disposal.

These pollutants may enter:

- Rivers
- Lakes
- Groundwater
- Oceans

Some pollutants can also enter the atmosphere, contributing to **acid rain**.

Industrial pollution harms aquatic ecosystems, reduces water quality, and may lead to **biomagnification** of toxic substances.

---

## Toxic Industrial Compounds

Many industries release harmful chemicals such as:

- Heavy metals
- Acids
- Industrial solvents
- Toxic organic compounds

If untreated waste enters water bodies, these substances can:

- Poison aquatic organisms.
- Reduce biodiversity.
- Enter food chains.
- Cause **biomagnification**, where toxin concentrations increase at higher trophic levels.

---

# 🌧️ Acid Rain

**Acid rain** is rainfall that has a lower pH than normal because it contains acidic pollutants.

It is mainly caused by burning **fossil fuels**, which release:

- **Sulfur dioxide (SO₂)**
- **Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ)**

These gases react with water vapour in the atmosphere to form acids.

When it rains, these acids fall back to Earth as **acid rain**.

---

## Formation of Acid Rain

The process can be summarised as:

1. Fossil fuels are burned.
2. Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides are released.
3. These gases react with water vapour in the atmosphere.
4. Acidic clouds form.
5. Acid rain falls onto land and water bodies.

---

## Effects of Acid Rain

Acid rain has serious environmental impacts.

### On Soil

- Damages soil structure.
- Reduces soil fertility.
- Leaches important mineral nutrients.

---

### On Plants

- Damages leaves.
- Reduces photosynthesis.
- Slows plant growth.
- Weakens forests.

---

### On Aquatic Life

Acid rain lowers the pH of lakes and rivers.

This can:

- Kill fish.
- Reduce fish breeding.
- Prevent eggs from hatching.
- Kill aquatic plants.

---

### Heavy Metal Leaching

Acid rain increases the movement of heavy metals from soil into water.

Examples include:

- **Aluminium**, which damages fish gills.
- **Lead**, which is highly toxic to aquatic organisms.

These metals also affect aquatic food chains.

---

### On Buildings

Acid rain reacts with materials such as limestone and marble.

Over time, it causes:

- Weathering.
- Surface erosion.
- Damage to historical buildings and monuments.

---

# 🌱 Reducing Acid Rain

Several methods can reduce acid rain.

These include:

- Reducing the use of fossil fuels.
- Increasing the use of renewable energy.
- Planting more trees (afforestation).
- Installing **flue gas desulfurisation units (scrubbers)** on factory chimneys.
- Enforcing strict pollution-control laws.
- Proper treatment of industrial waste before disposal.

---

## Quick Revision

- Industries release chemicals that pollute water.
- Toxic compounds can cause **biomagnification**.
- Acid rain is mainly caused by **SO₂** and **NOₓ** from burning fossil fuels.
- Acid rain damages soil, forests, aquatic ecosystems, and buildings.
- Pollution can be reduced through cleaner energy, emission controls, and stricter environmental laws.

---

### Exam Tip

> [!TIP]
> Learn the complete **acid rain sequence**:
>
> **Burning fossil fuels → SO₂ & NOₓ released → React with water vapour → Acid rain → Environmental damage**

---

### Common Exam Mistakes

> [!WARNING]
> - Saying acid rain is caused by **carbon dioxide**. Cambridge expects **sulfur dioxide (SO₂)** and **nitrogen oxides (NOₓ)**.
> - Confusing **acid rain** with **global warming**—they are different environmental issues.
> - Forgetting that acid rain can also damage **buildings and monuments**, not just plants and animals.
---

# 🌾 Agricultural Water Pollution

Agriculture is one of the largest sources of water pollution.

Modern farming relies heavily on:

- Fertilizers
- Pesticides
- Herbicides

When these chemicals are used excessively or incorrectly, rainwater can wash them into nearby rivers, lakes, and groundwater through **surface run-off** and **leaching**.

This pollutes freshwater ecosystems and harms aquatic organisms.

---

## Sources of Agricultural Pollution

### Fertilizers

Fertilizers contain important nutrients such as:

- Nitrates (NO₃⁻)
- Phosphates (PO₄³⁻)

These nutrients help crops grow but can become pollutants when washed into water bodies.

---

### Pesticides

Pesticides are chemicals used to kill insect pests.

If they enter rivers or lakes, they may:

- Poison aquatic organisms.
- Kill non-target species.
- Enter food chains and contribute to biomagnification.

---

### Herbicides

Herbicides are chemicals used to kill weeds.

Improper application can:

- Pollute nearby water bodies.
- Harm aquatic plants.
- Reduce biodiversity.

---

# 🌿 Eutrophication

**Eutrophication** is the enrichment of a water body with excessive nutrients, mainly **nitrates** and **phosphates**.

These nutrients commonly originate from:

- Agricultural fertilizers.
- Sewage.
- Industrial waste.
- Acid rain.

---

## Process of Eutrophication

The process occurs in several stages:

### Step 1 — Nutrient Enrichment

Large amounts of nitrates and phosphates enter rivers or lakes.

↓

### Step 2 — Algal Bloom

The excess nutrients cause rapid growth of algae, forming an **algal bloom**.

Some algal blooms may produce harmful toxins.

↓

### Step 3 — Sunlight is Blocked

The thick layer of algae prevents sunlight from reaching underwater plants.

As a result:

- Photosynthesis decreases.
- Aquatic plants begin to die.

↓

### Step 4 — Decomposition

Dead algae and aquatic plants are broken down by bacteria.

The bacteria multiply rapidly during decomposition.

↓

### Step 5 — Oxygen Levels Decrease

The bacteria use large amounts of dissolved oxygen during respiration.

This increases the **Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD)** of the water.

↓

### Step 6 — Death of Aquatic Organisms

Fish and other aquatic organisms cannot obtain enough oxygen.

Large numbers may die due to oxygen depletion.

---

## Effects of Eutrophication

Eutrophication can lead to:

- Rapid algal growth.
- Reduced light penetration.
- Death of aquatic plants.
- Increased Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD).
- Oxygen depletion.
- Fish kills.
- Loss of biodiversity.
- Poor water quality.

---

## Sources of Nutrients Causing Eutrophication

| Source | Nutrients Added |
| :--- | :--- |
| Agricultural fertilizers | Nitrates and phosphates |
| Sewage | Organic matter, nitrates and phosphates |
| Industrial waste | Nutrient-rich chemicals |
| Acid rain | Additional nitrogen compounds |

---

## Quick Revision

- Agriculture pollutes water through fertilizers, pesticides, and herbicides.
- Fertilizers mainly contain **nitrates** and **phosphates**.
- Excess nutrients cause **eutrophication**.
- Algal blooms block sunlight and increase BOD.
- Oxygen levels fall, causing fish and other aquatic organisms to die.

---

### Exam Tip

> [!TIP]
> Cambridge frequently asks students to **describe the stages of eutrophication**. Learn the sequence in order:
>
> **Excess nutrients → Algal bloom → Sunlight blocked → Plants die → Decomposition → Bacteria respire → Oxygen decreases → Fish die**

---

### Common Exam Mistakes

> [!WARNING]
> - Saying algae directly kill fish. Fish actually die because **bacteria use up dissolved oxygen** during decomposition.
> - Forgetting that **nitrates and phosphates** are the main nutrients responsible for eutrophication.
> - Confusing **eutrophication** with **water pollution**—eutrophication is **one consequence** of nutrient pollution.
---

# ⚖️ Pollution Control and Legislation

Reducing water pollution requires more than individual action.

Governments, industries, and international organizations work together by introducing **environmental laws (legislation)** and pollution-control strategies to protect freshwater resources.

These laws:

- Limit the amount of pollutants released into the environment.
- Protect rivers, lakes, and groundwater.
- Encourage industries to adopt cleaner technologies.
- Improve public health and conserve biodiversity.

---

# 🌊 Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement (GLWQA)

One important example of environmental legislation is the **Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement (GLWQA)**.

- **Established:** 1972
- **Countries involved:** Canada and the United States

The agreement was created to reduce pollution in the **Great Lakes**, one of the world's largest freshwater systems.

---

## Main Objectives

The agreement aimed to:

- Improve water quality.
- Reduce industrial and domestic pollution.
- Protect aquatic ecosystems.
- Prevent eutrophication.
- Encourage long-term environmental cooperation.

---

## Controlling Eutrophication

One of the biggest problems affecting the Great Lakes was **eutrophication**.

To reduce this problem:

- Strict limits were placed on **phosphorus** entering the lakes.
- Industries and communities were encouraged to reduce nutrient pollution.
- Water quality was monitored regularly.

Reducing phosphorus helped decrease algal blooms and improve aquatic ecosystems.

---

## Government Measures

Governments introduced several methods to reduce pollution.

### Penalties

Industries that exceeded pollution limits faced:

- Fines.
- Legal action.
- Stricter environmental regulations.

---

### Incentives

Industries that successfully reduced pollution were rewarded through:

- Tax relief.
- Government grants.
- Financial support for cleaner technologies.

This encouraged businesses to adopt environmentally friendly practices.

---

## Why Environmental Legislation is Important

Environmental laws help to:

- Protect drinking water supplies.
- Reduce pollution.
- Preserve biodiversity.
- Protect aquatic habitats.
- Promote sustainable development.
- Hold industries accountable for environmental damage.

Without legislation, many industries would have little incentive to reduce pollution.

---

## Quick Revision

- Pollution control depends on both **laws** and **responsible environmental management**.
- The **Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement (1972)** is an important example of international environmental cooperation.
- Phosphorus limits were introduced to reduce **eutrophication**.
- Governments may use both **penalties** and **financial incentives** to reduce pollution.

---

### Exam Tip

> [!TIP]
> Cambridge rarely asks for detailed facts about the **GLWQA**, but you should remember **why it was introduced** and **how controlling phosphorus helps reduce eutrophication**.

---

### Common Exam Mistakes

> [!WARNING]
> - Forgetting that legislation uses both **punishments (fines)** and **rewards (tax relief and grants)** to encourage pollution control.
> - Confusing the purpose of the GLWQA with climate change policies—it specifically focuses on **improving freshwater quality and reducing pollution**.

# 📚 Chapter Summary (Quick Revision)

This chapter covered the importance of water, its availability, uses, management, diseases, pollution, and methods used to protect this vital resource.

---

## 🌍 Water Distribution

- **71%** of Earth's surface is covered by water.
- Only **3%** is fresh water.
- About **2%** is locked in glaciers and ice caps.
- Only **1%** is readily available in rivers, lakes, streams, and wetlands.

---

## 💧 Water Cycle

Key processes include:

- Evaporation
- Condensation
- Precipitation
- Interception
- Surface run-off
- Infiltration
- Throughflow
- Groundwater flow
- Transpiration

---

## 🚰 Uses of Water

Water has three major uses:

- **Domestic** – drinking, cooking, washing, sanitation.
- **Industrial** – cooling, manufacturing, chemical processes.
- **Agricultural** – irrigation, livestock, cleaning.

> Agriculture uses **approximately 70%** of global freshwater.

---

## 💦 Sources of Fresh Water

- Rivers
- Lakes
- Reservoirs
- Groundwater
- Aquifers
- Desalinated seawater

**Desalination methods:**

- Distillation
- Reverse osmosis

---

## 🌍 Water Scarcity

Two main types:

| Physical Water Scarcity | Economic Water Scarcity |
| :--- | :--- |
| Not enough natural freshwater. | Water exists but cannot be accessed because of poor infrastructure or lack of money. |

---

## 🏞️ Multipurpose Dams

### Advantages

- Hydroelectric power
- Irrigation
- Flood control
- Drinking water
- Tourism
- Employment

### Disadvantages

- Displacement of people
- Fish migration disrupted
- Silt accumulation
- Reduced downstream sediment
- Possible seismic activity

---

## 🦠 Water-related Diseases

| Disease | Type | Cause |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cholera | Water-borne | Contaminated water |
| Typhoid | Water-borne | Contaminated water |
| Malaria | Water-related | *Plasmodium* parasite transmitted by mosquitoes |

---

## 🏭 Water Pollution

Main sources include:

- Domestic waste (sewage)
- Industrial waste
- Agricultural chemicals

---

## 🧪 Sewage Treatment

Main stages:

1. Screening
2. Primary sedimentation
3. Aeration
4. Secondary sedimentation
5. Effluent discharge
6. Sludge digestion
7. Tertiary treatment (optional)

---

## 🌧️ Acid Rain

Caused mainly by:

- Sulfur dioxide (SO₂)
- Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ)

Major impacts:

- Damages forests.
- Acidifies lakes and rivers.
- Kills aquatic organisms.
- Damages buildings.
- Causes heavy metal leaching.

---

## 🌿 Eutrophication

Sequence:

> Excess nutrients → Algal bloom → Sunlight blocked → Plants die → Bacteria decompose → Oxygen decreases → Fish die

Main nutrients involved:

- Nitrates
- Phosphates

---

## ⚖️ Pollution Control

The **Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement (1972)** aimed to:

- Reduce phosphorus pollution.
- Prevent eutrophication.
- Improve freshwater quality.
- Encourage international cooperation.

Governments use:

- Environmental laws
- Fines
- Tax incentives
- Pollution monitoring

---

## 🎯 Key Facts to Remember

- Earth is **71% water**, but only **1%** is readily available freshwater.
- Agriculture is the largest consumer of freshwater.
- Reverse osmosis is generally more efficient than distillation.
- Physical and economic water scarcity are different concepts.
- Multipurpose dams have both advantages and disadvantages.
- Cholera and typhoid are **water-borne**, while malaria is **water-related**.
- Sewage treatment reduces **Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD)**.
- Acid rain is mainly caused by **SO₂** and **NOₓ**.
- Eutrophication is caused by excess **nitrates** and **phosphates**.
- Pollution control requires both legislation and responsible environmental management.

# 📝 Key Comparison Tables

## 1. Types of Water Scarcity

| Physical Water Scarcity | Economic Water Scarcity |
| :--- | :--- |
| Caused by a lack of natural freshwater resources. | Caused by poor infrastructure or lack of investment. |
| Common in deserts and dry climates. | Can occur even where water is abundant. |
| Linked to environmental conditions. | Linked to economic development and management. |
| Example: UAE, Kuwait | Example: Many developing countries |

---

## 2. Distillation vs Reverse Osmosis

| Distillation | Reverse Osmosis |
| :--- | :--- |
| Water is boiled and condensed. | Water is forced through fine membranes under pressure. |
| High energy requirement. | Lower energy requirement. |
| More expensive. | Less expensive. |
| Produces more brine. | Produces less brine. |
| Less efficient. | More efficient. |

---

## 3. Water-borne vs Water-related Diseases

| Water-borne Diseases | Water-related Diseases |
| :--- | :--- |
| Spread through contaminated water. | Spread by vectors associated with water. |
| Caused by drinking polluted water. | Caused by mosquito bites. |
| Examples: Cholera, Typhoid | Example: Malaria |
| Usually bacterial diseases. | Caused by the *Plasmodium* parasite. |

---

## 4. Surface Water vs Groundwater

| Surface Water | Groundwater |
| :--- | :--- |
| Found on Earth's surface. | Stored underground in aquifers. |
| Examples: Rivers, lakes, streams. | Extracted using wells and pumps. |
| Easier to access. | Usually cleaner but harder to extract. |

---

## 5. Multipurpose Dams

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| :--- | :--- |
| Generate hydroelectric power. | Displace people. |
| Control floods. | Disrupt fish migration. |
| Supply irrigation water. | Trap sediment and silt. |
| Provide drinking water. | Alter downstream river flow. |
| Support tourism and recreation. | May increase seismic activity. |

---

## 6. Sources of Water Pollution

| Source | Main Pollutants | Major Effects |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Domestic Waste | Sewage, organic matter | High BOD, spread of disease |
| Industry | Toxic chemicals, heavy metals | Biomagnification, acid rain |
| Agriculture | Fertilisers, pesticides, herbicides | Eutrophication, water contamination |

---

## 7. Potable Water Treatment vs Sewage Treatment

| Potable Water Treatment | Sewage Treatment |
| :--- | :--- |
| Produces safe drinking water. | Treats wastewater before discharge. |
| Includes coagulation, filtration and chlorination. | Includes screening, sedimentation, aeration and sludge digestion. |
| Removes pathogens from clean water sources. | Removes pollutants from sewage. |

---

## 8. Causes and Effects of Acid Rain

| Causes | Effects |
| :--- | :--- |
| Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) | Damages forests |
| Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) | Acidifies lakes and rivers |
| Burning fossil fuels | Kills aquatic organisms |
| Industrial emissions | Damages buildings and monuments |
| Vehicle exhausts | Causes heavy metal leaching |

---

## 9. Eutrophication Sequence

| Stage | What Happens |
| :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Excess nitrates and phosphates enter water. |
| 2 | Algal bloom develops rapidly. |
| 3 | Sunlight is blocked. |
| 4 | Aquatic plants die. |
| 5 | Bacteria decompose dead material. |
| 6 | Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD) increases. |
| 7 | Dissolved oxygen decreases. |
| 8 | Fish and other aquatic organisms die. |

---

## 10. Important Cambridge Definitions

| Term | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| Aquifer | A layer of porous rock that stores groundwater. |
| Potable Water | Water that is safe for human consumption. |
| Desalination | Removal of dissolved salts from seawater. |
| Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD) | Amount of oxygen required by microorganisms to decompose organic matter. |
| Vector | An organism that carries and transmits a disease-causing organism. |
| Eutrophication | Enrichment of water with nutrients leading to algal blooms and oxygen depletion. |
| Acid Rain | Rainfall made acidic by sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides. |
| Effluent | Treated wastewater released into the environment. |
| Sludge | Solid waste removed during sewage treatment. |
| Reservoir | A large store of water, usually created behind a dam. |

# 💡 Exam Tips

### ✅ Learn the Water Cycle in Sequence

Be able to explain the complete water cycle in order:

> **Evaporation → Condensation → Precipitation → Infiltration → Throughflow / Groundwater Flow → Rivers → Oceans**

Know the meaning of each process.

---

### ✅ Know the Difference Between Water Scarcity Types

Students are often asked to distinguish between:

- Physical water scarcity
- Economic water scarcity

Always include **both the cause and an example** if possible.

---

### ✅ Memorise Desalination Methods

Know how each method works and be able to compare them.

Focus on:

- Process
- Cost
- Energy requirement
- Efficiency
- Waste produced

---

### ✅ Learn Advantages *and* Disadvantages of Dams

Cambridge evaluation questions often ask:

> *"Assess the impacts of multipurpose dams."*

Always provide:

- Environmental impacts
- Social impacts
- Economic impacts

to score higher marks.

---

### ✅ Understand Disease Transmission

Remember:

- **Cholera & Typhoid** → Water-borne
- **Malaria** → Water-related (mosquito vector)

Do **not** confuse contaminated water with mosquito transmission.

---

### ✅ Master Sewage Treatment

Learn the stages in order:

1. Screening
2. Primary sedimentation
3. Aeration
4. Secondary sedimentation
5. Effluent discharge
6. Sludge digestion
7. Tertiary treatment (if required)

Questions often ask:

- Why oxygen is bubbled into sewage.
- Why methane is produced.
- What happens to sludge.

---

### ✅ Memorise the Formation of Acid Rain

Remember this sequence:

> **Burning fossil fuels → SO₂ & NOₓ released → React with water vapour → Acid rain → Environmental damage**

This process is commonly tested.

---

### ✅ Learn Eutrophication as a Process

Instead of memorising individual facts, remember the complete chain:

> **Nutrients → Algal bloom → Light blocked → Plants die → Decomposition → BOD increases → Oxygen decreases → Fish die**

# ❌ Common Exam Mistakes

### Water Distribution

- Saying **3%** of Earth's water is easily available.
- Forgetting that most freshwater is locked in glaciers.

---

### Water Cycle

- Confusing **evaporation** with **transpiration**.
- Mixing up **infiltration**, **throughflow**, and **groundwater flow**.

---

### Water Scarcity

- Confusing physical scarcity with economic scarcity.
- Assuming high rainfall means everyone has safe drinking water.

---

### Desalination

- Forgetting that **reverse osmosis** is generally **more efficient** than distillation.
- Mixing up the two desalination methods.

---

### Dams

- Writing only the advantages.
- Ignoring environmental and social impacts.

---

### Water-related Diseases

- Saying malaria spreads through dirty water.
- Forgetting that mosquitoes are **vectors**.

---

### Sewage Treatment

- Confusing sewage treatment with potable water treatment.
- Forgetting that **aerobic bacteria require oxygen**.
- Mixing up **sludge** and **effluent**.

---

### Acid Rain

- Writing **carbon dioxide** instead of **sulfur dioxide (SO₂)** and **nitrogen oxides (NOₓ)**.
- Confusing acid rain with global warming.

---

### Eutrophication

- Saying algae directly kill fish.
- Forgetting that fish die because **decomposer bacteria consume dissolved oxygen**.
